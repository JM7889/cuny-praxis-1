Praxis I — Follow the Money: Exploring CUNY Through Financial Data

Introduction

I decided to revisit my first praxis assignment because I now have a better understanding of what we are exploring in this class. My original project examined research funding and how a single large contribution could dominate a visualization. This time, I wanted to focus less on the financial findings themselves and more on how different ways of presenting data influence what we see.

I chose Apache Superset, an open-source visualization platform, because of its flexibility and wide range of interactive tools. Using CUNY's audited financial statements for 2024 and 2025, I created a dashboard comparing funding sources, expenditures, and changes between years.

My objective was simple: explore how the same information can tell different stories depending on how we organize and display it.

What I Learned

The most interesting result was how strongly visualization choices influence interpretation.

For example, CUNY reported approximately $6.49 billion in revenue and $6 billion in expenses in 2025. New York State appropriations represented the largest individual revenue category. In a donut chart, this category dominates the display, making smaller sources difficult to distinguish. Removing it makes the others more visible without changing their actual values.

Different graphs also serve different purposes. Bar charts make numerical comparisons easier, while proportional-area displays communicate relative size. Tables are less visually appealing but provide greater precision.

This experiment connects with Mark Monmonier's How to Lie with Maps, particularly his discussion of how scale and representation influence interpretation. It also relates to D'Ignazio and Klein's Data Feminism, which asks us to consider what gets counted, how categories are constructed, and what remains invisible.

Financial records illustrate these questions particularly well. They tell us how institutions account for their activities, but not necessarily how resources are experienced or used. We can identify CUNY's spending on instruction or research without knowing how those resources are distributed among individual departments or whether they meet the needs of students and faculty.

The Tools and Their Limitations

Superset worked well for comparing financial information through different visualizations. Being open source is another advantage, particularly for a project that I hope to expand and share.

However, getting there was more complicated than the finished dashboard suggests. Preparing the data, configuring Docker and PostgreSQL, connecting Superset, and troubleshooting the charts required considerably more work than I initially expected.

I also created a standalone version and a reproduction manual to make the experiment easier to share.

The financial data itself has limitations. It covers only two fiscal years and represents CUNY as a consolidated institution. It cannot establish detailed spending by college or department, nor can it tell us whether greater spending produces better educational outcomes.

Conclusion

I began this project wanting to understand CUNY's finances. I ended up learning more about how visualization influences the way we understand information.

The same dataset can emphasize different relationships depending on the graph, scale, classification, or filters we choose. None of these decisions is entirely neutral.

I also came to appreciate how much work happens behind a seemingly simple visualization, from collecting and organizing data to making technical decisions about how it should be presented.

My main takeaway is that visualization is not just a way of communicating research. It is part of the research process itself.

This exercise is also a starting point for a broader investigation of CUNY's finances and digital infrastructure, but for now, its main value is in understanding how our methods shape what we are able to see.
